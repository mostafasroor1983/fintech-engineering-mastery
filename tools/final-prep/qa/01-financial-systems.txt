## Money and precision
Q: How do you represent money in a Java payment system, and why?
A: As an integer count of minor units (`long` pence/cents) or a `BigDecimal` with the currency's scale, always paired with an ISO 4217 currency code in a value object. Never `double`: binary floating point cannot represent 0.1 exactly and errors accumulate. *Trade-off:* `long` is fast and exact but you must know each currency's exponent (JPY has 0, KWD has 3); `BigDecimal` is flexible but slower and `equals` compares scale (`1.0` ≠ `1.00`), so compare with `compareTo`.

Q: You must split £100 between three parties. What goes wrong and how do you do it correctly?
A: Naive division gives 33.33 × 3 = 99.99: a penny disappears. Use allocation by largest remainder: compute each floor share, then hand the leftover minor units one by one to the largest remainders, so shares sum exactly to the original (34, 33, 33 pence for £1). Document the tie-break rule; auditors will ask who gets the extra penny and why.

Q: Where should rounding happen in a fee or FX calculation?
A: Once, at a defined boundary, with an explicit rounding mode written into the product rule (for example, fees rounded half-up, customer FX amounts rounded down). Keep full precision through intermediate steps (`BigDecimal` with `MathContext`), never round twice. *Failure mode:* rounding in several services independently creates or destroys money that reconciliation later flags as unexplained breaks.

Q: Why is `new BigDecimal(0.1)` a bug?
A: It converts the already-imprecise binary double, giving 0.1000000000000000055511151231257827…. Use `new BigDecimal("0.1")` or `BigDecimal.valueOf(0.1)` (which goes through `Double.toString`). In money code, avoid doubles entirely at the boundary: parse amounts from strings or integers.

## Ledgers and accounting
Q: Explain double-entry bookkeeping to an engineer in one minute.
A: Every movement of money is a journal entry with at least two postings, debits and credits, that sum to zero per currency. Balances are derived by summing postings, so money cannot appear or vanish without a matching entry. For engineers: it is an invariant (`sum(postings) == 0`) enforced at write time, plus an append-only log that makes every balance explainable.

Q: Why should a ledger be append-only, and how do you fix a mistaken entry?
A: Auditability: regulators and finance must reconstruct any balance at any point in time. Corrections are new entries: a reversal referencing the original, then the correct entry, each with a reason and actor. Enforce it with database privileges (no UPDATE/DELETE grants on postings) or triggers, not just code review.

Q: Should you store a balance column if balances are derived from postings?
A: Yes, as a cache for fast reads and for balance checks under locks, updated in the same transaction as the postings. Treat it as derived: a reconciliation job compares it with `sum(postings)` and alerts on drift. Never "fix" a balance with a direct UPDATE; post a correcting entry.

Q: How do you enforce that every journal entry balances in PostgreSQL?
A: A deferred constraint trigger (`DEFERRABLE INITIALLY DEFERRED`) that runs at COMMIT and checks `sum(amount) = 0` grouped by currency for the entry. Row-level CHECK constraints can't see sibling rows, and an immediate trigger fires before the second posting exists. Keep the application check too, for clear errors.

Q: What is the difference between available balance and ledger (book) balance?
A: Ledger balance is the sum of settled postings. Available balance subtracts holds and pending debits (card authorisations, reserved funds) and may add an overdraft limit. Spending decisions use available balance; statements and interest use ledger balance. Mixing them up allows double spending against funds already reserved.

Q: How do you handle hot accounts, such as a merchant settlement account receiving thousands of credits per second?
A: Options: keep transactions tiny; split the account into N sub-accounts and credit one at random (sum for the balance); or buffer credits in an append-only table and fold them into the balance asynchronously, since credits need no balance check. Avoid optimistic locking on the hot row: contention turns into a retry storm.

Q: How would you design multi-currency balances?
A: One balance per (account, currency); never a single balance converted on the fly. FX conversion is a journal entry with four postings through FX position accounts: debit and credit in each currency, each currency balancing separately. Revaluation for reporting is a separate accounting process, not a change to customer balances.

## Payments lifecycle
Q: Walk through a card payment from tap to settlement.
A: Authorisation: the acquirer sends the request through the scheme to the issuer, which approves and places a hold. Capture/clearing: the merchant submits the final amount (often in a batch), the scheme clears it and the issuer posts the transaction. Settlement: the scheme nets positions and moves funds between issuer and acquirer, and the acquirer pays the merchant minus fees, typically T+1 or T+2. Chargebacks can follow weeks or months later.

Q: What is the difference between authorisation and capture, and what edge cases matter?
A: Authorisation reserves funds; capture asks for them. Edge cases: partial capture (shipping part of an order), capture after the authorisation expired (may decline or need re-authorisation), incremental authorisation (hotels, car hire), reversal of an uncaptured authorisation, and capture amount above the authorised amount (only allowed in specific scheme/MCC cases such as tips). Model it as a state machine with amount invariants.

Q: The call to your payment provider times out. What state is the payment in?
A: Unknown, not failed. The provider may have processed it. Record it as PENDING/UNKNOWN, return 202 to the client, retry with the same idempotency key or query the provider's status endpoint, and let end-of-day reconciliation be the safety net. Marking it failed invites the customer to pay again: the classic double charge.

Q: How do you guarantee a customer is never charged twice when they click "Pay" twice or the client retries?
A: Client-generated idempotency key per payment attempt, scoped per merchant, stored with a hash of the request under a unique constraint in the same transaction as the payment intent; replay the stored response for duplicates; reject the same key with a different body. Forward the same key to the PSP. Duplicate detection by "same amount within 5 minutes" is a business heuristic, not a guarantee.

Q: What is a chargeback and what does your system need to support it?
A: The cardholder disputes a transaction through their issuer; the scheme moves funds back from the merchant side pending evidence. You need: a link from the dispute to the original payment (by scheme reference) even months later, a dispute state machine with deadlines, ledger entries for the provisional debit and its reversal if won, and evidence storage. Chargeback ratios above scheme thresholds trigger monitoring programmes and fines.

Q: What are refunds versus reversals versus chargebacks?
A: A reversal cancels an authorisation or a transaction before settlement (no money moved yet). A refund is a new, merchant-initiated credit after settlement. A chargeback is cardholder/issuer-initiated through the scheme dispute process. They differ in who initiates, timing, fees and ledger entries; never model a refund as "delete the payment".

Q: What does "real-time payments" (Faster Payments, SEPA Instant, FedNow, UPI) change in your design?
A: Settlement is final within seconds and cannot be recalled freely, so fraud and limit checks must happen before submission, within a tight latency budget. Availability is 24/7/365, so no batch windows or maintenance downtime. Timeouts still leave unknown states: query the scheme by end-to-end id, never resend with a new id. Liquidity at the settlement account must be monitored in real time.

Q: What is ISO 20022 and why does it matter to engineers?
A: The XML/JSON financial messaging standard: pacs.008 (customer credit transfer), pacs.002 (status report), camt.053 (statement), camt.054 (notification), pain.001 (customer initiation). It carries structured, richer data (structured addresses, purpose codes, end-to-end ids) than SWIFT MT. Engineers must map it carefully, validate against schemas, parse XML safely (disable external entities) and keep the end-to-end id through every hop for reconciliation.

Q: What is a PSP, an acquirer, an issuer and a scheme?
A: The issuer is the cardholder's bank; the acquirer is the merchant's bank that accepts card payments; the scheme (Visa, Mastercard) runs the network and rules; a PSP/payment gateway gives merchants a single API over acquirers and methods. Knowing who holds the money and the liability at each step explains where your system must reconcile.

## Settlement and reconciliation
Q: What is reconciliation and why is it a first-class system, not a report?
A: Comparing two independent records of the same money (ledger vs bank statement, ledger vs PSP settlement report) and explaining every difference. It is the proof that the system works: it catches lost events, duplicate postings, timing differences and fraud. It needs ingestion, matching rules, typed breaks, a workflow with owners and SLAs, and audit, so it is a product in its own right.

Q: How do you design a matching engine for bank statement lines against ledger entries?
A: Pass 1: exact keys (end-to-end id, bank reference). Pass 2: amount + currency + value date within a tolerance, only when there is exactly one candidate. Many-to-one matches (batched payouts) as a separate rule. Never auto-match ambiguous candidates; send them to a human. Record which rule matched, so you can audit and tune.

Q: What types of reconciliation breaks do you expect, and how do you handle each?
A: Timing (in ledger, not yet at bank): auto-resolve when it appears within tolerance. Missing in ledger (bank debit we didn't expect: fees, chargebacks): create an entry after investigation. Missing at bank: investigate rejected or lost payments. Amount mismatch: fees netted, FX, partial. Duplicates. Alert by age and amount, not on every break, or the team ignores alerts.

Q: Your settlement file from the bank arrives twice. What happens?
A: Nothing, if ingestion is idempotent: store the file's SHA-256 (and the bank's file/sequence id) under a unique constraint and return the previous result for a duplicate. Also validate the file: opening balance + entries = closing balance, sequence continuity with the previous file. A corrected re-send needs an explicit, audited replace path.

Q: What is netting in settlement and what risk does it reduce?
A: Instead of settling each payment gross, participants exchange only the net difference per period. It reduces liquidity needs and the number of transfers, but adds settlement risk: if a participant fails before settlement, the netted positions must be unwound. Real-time gross settlement (RTGS) avoids that risk at the cost of liquidity.

## Wallets, FX and lending
Q: Design the data model for a multi-currency wallet.
A: `account(id, owner, currency, status, version)`, `journal_entry(id, idempotency_key UNIQUE, type, created_at)`, `posting(entry_id, account_id, amount_minor signed, currency)`, a balance cache per account updated in the same transaction, and `hold` rows for reserved funds. One account per currency. Constraints: entry balances per currency (deferred trigger), balance ≥ 0 where overdraft isn't allowed.

Q: How do you implement an FX quote that the customer can accept for 30 seconds?
A: Create a quote row with pair, rate, sell and buy amounts, expiry and `used_at NULL`. Acceptance is one conditional update: `UPDATE fx_quote SET used_at = now() WHERE id = :id AND used_at IS NULL AND expires_at > now()`; one row updated means accepted, then post the four-leg ledger entry in the same transaction. Stop quoting when market data is stale (circuit breaker on feed age).

Q: How do you calculate interest on balances correctly?
A: Daily accrual on the end-of-day ledger balance using the product's day-count convention (ACT/365, ACT/360, 30/360), keeping accruals at high precision in a separate accrual account, and posting (capitalising) at the period end with one rounding. Rate changes apply from an effective date. Backdated transactions require recalculation of accruals from that date, so keep accruals reproducible.

Q: What is a hold (reservation) and how do you make it expire safely?
A: A record that reduces available balance without moving money, created at authorisation. Expiry job selects expired holds with `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`, releases them with a conditional status update, and is idempotent. Race: a capture arriving while the hold expires; resolve with the same row lock or a conditional update on status, and decide by rule which wins.

## Risk, fraud and compliance
Q: How do you fit fraud scoring into an authorisation with a 100 ms budget?
A: Synchronous scoring on pre-computed features (one fast lookup in an online feature store), rules plus a model, under a strict timeout and circuit breaker; asynchronous feature computation from the event stream. When scoring is slow or down, apply a pre-agreed stand-in policy by amount and risk segment; neither approve-all nor decline-all.

Q: What are KYC and AML, and how do they show up in system design?
A: KYC (know your customer) verifies identity at onboarding and periodically; AML (anti-money laundering) monitors transactions for suspicious patterns and screens against sanctions lists. In systems: onboarding states that block functionality until verified, screening calls in payment paths (with timeouts and a hold-for-review state), transaction monitoring on the event stream, case management, and immutable audit of every decision.

Q: How does PCI DSS affect a backend architecture?
A: Minimise cardholder-data scope: tokenise card numbers at the edge (or use a PSP's hosted fields), keep raw PANs only in a small, isolated, hardened vault service, never log them, encrypt at rest with managed keys, and restrict network paths. Everything that touches raw PANs is in scope for audit, so scope reduction is the main architectural lever.

Q: What does PSD2 strong customer authentication mean for a payments API?
A: In the EEA/UK, many electronic payments need two independent factors (knowledge, possession, inherence), with exemptions (low value, trusted beneficiaries, transaction risk analysis). Your flow must support a challenge step (3-D Secure for cards, redirect/decoupled for open banking), store the exemption applied, and handle the payment staying pending during the challenge.

Q: What is the difference between sanctions screening and transaction monitoring?
A: Screening checks parties against sanctions/PEP lists before a payment proceeds (synchronous, block or hold on a hit). Monitoring analyses behaviour over time (velocity, structuring, unusual corridors) and raises alerts for investigation (asynchronous). Screening false positives need a fast human review workflow, or legitimate payments stall.

Q: Why must financial systems keep an immutable audit trail, and what goes into it?
A: Regulators, auditors and dispute handling need to know who did what, when, why and from where, and to prove records were not altered. Each event: actor, action, target, before/after or the command, timestamp, correlation id, reason. Store append-only (no update/delete grants; WORM storage or hash chaining for tamper evidence). Application logs are not an audit trail: they rotate, sample and can be edited.

Q: How long must you keep financial records, and how does that affect design?
A: Typically 5–10 years depending on jurisdiction and record type (e.g. AML rules often require 5 years after the relationship ends). Design: time-partitioned tables, archival to cheap immutable storage with a query path, legal holds that block deletion, and crypto-shredding or anonymisation where privacy law requires deletion of personal data that isn't legally required.

Q: GDPR right to erasure conflicts with financial record retention. How do you reconcile them?
A: Legal obligations to retain records override erasure for that data, but only for what is required. Separate personal data from transaction records (reference a customer id), delete or anonymise what isn't required, and use crypto-shredding (per-customer encryption keys deleted on erasure) for data held in immutable stores. Document the legal basis per data class.

## Operations and reliability in finance
Q: What does end-of-day (EOD) processing mean in a 24/7 system?
A: There is no quiet window, so EOD becomes a logical cut-off: a business date that switches at a defined time, with transactions stamped with both timestamp and business date. Batch jobs (interest accrual, statements, settlement files) run against the closed business date while new transactions continue on the next one. Never lock tables for EOD.

Q: How do time zones and business dates cause bugs in payments?
A: Cut-off times are defined in the bank's or scheme's local time, holidays differ per country, and DST changes shift UTC offsets. Store instants in UTC, compute business dates explicitly with the right calendar and zone, and test around DST switches, month ends and holidays. A classic bug: a payment at 23:30 local time posted to the wrong business date because the server used UTC.

Q: How do you test a payment system for correctness, not just happy paths?
A: Property-based tests on invariants (ledger always balances, balance equals sum of postings), concurrency tests with many threads on the same accounts, idempotency tests with concurrent duplicate requests, fault injection (PSP timeout, DB failover, Kafka down), contract tests with providers, and reconciliation run in staging against simulated bank files.

Q: What SLIs would you choose for a payments platform?
A: Payment success rate (excluding customer declines), API p99 latency, time to finality, rate of payments stuck in UNKNOWN beyond N minutes, reconciliation breaks by age and value, ledger invariant violations (must be zero), and duplicate-charge count (must be zero). Business-meaningful SLIs beat CPU graphs in an incident.

Q: A merchant reports customers charged twice yesterday. How do you investigate?
A: Mitigate first: identify affected payments (same customer, amount, close timestamps, different payment ids) and start refunds. Then trace: were the idempotency keys different (client bug) or the same (our bug)? Check logs and the idempotency table for the request ids, PSP references and retry patterns. Fix the root cause, add a test reproducing it, and a monitor for duplicates.

Q: Why do financial systems prefer strong consistency on the write path but tolerate eventual consistency elsewhere?
A: Balance checks and postings must see the latest committed state, or you allow overdrafts and double spending. Statements, analytics, search and notifications can lag by seconds without harm, as long as they show an "as of" time and critical decisions read from the source of truth. Choosing per use case keeps the expensive guarantee only where money is at risk.

Q: What is a suspense (clearing) account and when do you use it?
A: A temporary account where money sits while a process is in progress or unexplained: a cross-shard transfer midway through a saga, an incoming payment that can't be matched to a customer, a payout awaiting confirmation. It keeps every entry balanced while making the in-between state visible and reconcilable. Monitor its age and balance: money must not stay there.

Q: How do you migrate balances from a legacy core banking system without losing money?
A: Freeze or dual-run: migrate opening balances as journal entries against a migration account, replay transactions after the snapshot, reconcile every account (old vs new) to zero difference before switching, run both in parallel with shadow comparison for a period, and keep a tested rollback. Migrate by cohort, not big bang.

Q: What is the "four-eyes" principle and how do you implement it?
A: Sensitive actions (large manual adjustments, limit changes, write-offs) need a maker and a different checker. Implement a pending-approval state, record both identities, prevent self-approval in code and by role, and audit. Also time-bound approvals and re-approval if the request changes.

Q: How do you handle negative balances caused by chargebacks or fees after the customer has spent the money?
A: The ledger must allow them for specific account types (it's a real debt), with a recovery workflow: block outgoing payments, notify, collect from the next incoming funds, eventually write off with approval. Hard `balance >= 0` constraints apply to customer-initiated debits, not to scheme-initiated ones you can't refuse.

Q: How would you explain the difference between a payment being authorised, settled and final?
A: Authorised: the issuer approved and reserved funds; nothing moved. Settled: the funds moved between institutions in the settlement system. Final: the transfer can no longer be unwound by the system's rules (instant schemes are final at settlement; card payments remain disputable via chargebacks long after). Product promises ("money received") must match the real state.

Q: How would you route payments across several PSPs or acquirers?
A: A routing layer chooses a provider per payment by rules (currency, card type, country, cost) and live health (success rate, latency, circuit-breaker state). Failover only for failures that are certain not to have charged (connection refused, explicit decline codes that allow retry); a timeout is unknown and must not be resent to a second provider. Every attempt is a separate record linked to one payment, with its own idempotency key.

Q: Design merchant payouts (paying out the merchant's balance daily).
A: A scheduled job per merchant computes the payable balance as of the cut-off (settled funds minus reserves and pending refunds), creates a payout record with an idempotency key, posts the ledger entry (merchant balance to payout-in-transit), submits to the bank rail, and on confirmation moves in-transit to settled. Failures return funds with a reversal entry. Reserves and rolling holds protect against later chargebacks.
