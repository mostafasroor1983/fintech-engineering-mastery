## System design framing
- Spend the first five minutes on requirements and say them back: functional, non-functional (latency, availability, consistency), and what's out of scope.
- Ask "can this operation be executed twice?" early in any payments design; it shows you know where fintech systems break.
- State assumptions aloud with numbers ("assume 10 million users, 2 payments each per day") so the interviewer can correct them before you build on them.
- Do the estimation arithmetic on the board; the method matters more than the exact number.
- Convert daily volume to per-second by dividing by about 100,000, then apply a stated peak factor.
- Let the estimate drive a decision ("this fits one PostgreSQL primary"); an estimate that changes nothing wastes time.
- Draw the data model before the boxes: tables, keys and constraints reveal correctness thinking.
- Put money in minor units with a currency column in your schema sketch; interviewers notice.
- Separate the synchronous accept path from asynchronous processing in your first diagram.
- Name the source of truth for every piece of data; ambiguity there is where consistency bugs hide.
- Define the API with idempotency keys, status codes and error format; it's quick and high-signal.
- Reach the deep dive by minute 25 of 45; if you're still drawing boxes, cut the diagram to five components.
- Pick the deep dive where money can be lost or duplicated, or ask the interviewer which part matters most to them.
- Walk the failure path, not just the happy path: "the PSP times out; here's what happens."
- Add a failure-mode table (failure, detection, mitigation, blast radius) near the end; it summarises resilience thinking.
- Mention SLIs and SLOs for the system you designed; it shows you think about operating it.
- Close with a 60-second summary and the risks you would tackle next with more time.
- When the interviewer changes a requirement mid-way, restate the change and show what it breaks in your design.
- If you don't know a specific technology, describe the mechanism you need instead ("a log with per-key ordering").
- Use the interviewer as a collaborator: "Would you like me to go deeper on consistency or on scaling?"
- Keep diagrams legible: label arrows with what flows (command, event, query) and sync vs async.
- Write the read and write paths separately when they have different scale or consistency needs.
- Show how a request is traced end to end (correlation id) when you discuss operations.
- Mention security boundaries early: who authenticates, where authorisation happens, where card data lives.
- For multi-region questions, ask about data residency and RPO/RTO before choosing active-active.
- Prefer boring, proven technology in your design and justify any exotic choice with a requirement.
- Say what you would measure before optimising; it signals engineering discipline.
- When asked "how would this scale 10×?", find the first bottleneck rather than listing every scaling technique.
- Don't design for a billion users when the requirement is a hundred thousand; over-engineering is a signal too.
- Practise the round on a clock; timing problems are invisible until you rehearse under pressure.

## Architecture trade-offs
- Phrase every choice as "X because Y; the cost is Z; I'd revisit if W."
- Compare at least two options on the dimensions that matter for this problem, not generic pros and cons.
- Strong consistency where money moves, eventual consistency where it's only shown: say which is which.
- A modular monolith is a legitimate senior answer when the domain is young and the team is small.
- Microservices trade single-database transactions for independence; name what replaces those transactions (sagas, outbox, reconciliation).
- Synchronous calls couple availability; events couple schemas. Say which coupling you accept and why.
- Caching buys latency at the price of staleness; never cache what a spending decision depends on.
- Sharding buys write scale at the price of cross-shard transactions; choose the key so most operations stay single-shard.
- Kafka is a log, not a queue: it gives replay and ordering per partition, not per-message acknowledgement and priorities.
- Read replicas scale reads but add lag; keep post-write reads and balance checks on the primary.
- Event sourcing gives a perfect audit history but makes queries, schema evolution and erasure harder; use it where history is the product.
- CQRS is worth it when read and write shapes really differ; otherwise it's two models to maintain.
- Optimistic locking suits low contention; pessimistic locking suits hot rows. Name the contention you expect.
- Exactly-once delivery doesn't exist across networks; exactly-once effects do, with idempotency.
- Sagas give availability but visible intermediate states; mention semantic locks or holds to manage them.
- A workflow engine (Temporal) earns its keep when you have many long-running processes; for one simple flow, a persisted state machine is fine.
- Distributed SQL buys consistency at scale with consensus latency; sharded PostgreSQL keeps latency low but pushes complexity into your code.
- Build vs buy: buy commodity (KYC checks, email), build differentiators, and keep an interface so you can switch.
- Every new technology adds operational cost; count on-call, upgrades and expertise in your trade-off.
- Timeouts trade completeness for availability; a fast failure is usually better than a slow success nobody waits for.
- Retries trade load for success rate; cap them with budgets and make the operation idempotent first.
- Fail open or fail closed depends on whether the control protects fairness or safety; say which.
- Active-active multi-region trades simplicity and consistency for availability; active-passive is often enough for a ledger.
- Denormalising for reads is fine if one place owns the truth and the copies are rebuildable.
- Mention reversibility: prefer decisions that are cheap to undo, and spend design time on the ones that aren't.
- Use ADRs to show decisions are recorded with context; it's a staff-level habit.
- Explain how you'd validate a trade-off with a prototype or load test instead of debating it.
- Consider the team: a design the team can't operate is worse than a simpler one it can.
- Data gravity matters: compute moving to data is often cheaper than data moving to compute.
- Name the constraint you're optimising for (latency, cost, correctness, time to market) before choosing.

## Fintech correctness
- Never use `double` for money; say minor units or `BigDecimal` with explicit rounding, and why.
- Allocation must not lose a cent: mention the largest-remainder method for splits.
- Round once, at a defined boundary, with a documented rule.
- Every money movement is a balanced journal entry; balances are derived and cached, never edited directly.
- Corrections are reversals plus new entries, with a reason and an actor.
- A timeout from a PSP or bank is an unknown outcome; say "UNKNOWN, resolve by status query and reconciliation."
- Idempotency keys belong in the same transaction as the business change, under a unique constraint.
- Same idempotency key with a different body must be rejected, not replayed.
- Reconciliation is the proof the system works; describe matching passes and typed breaks.
- Never auto-match ambiguous reconciliation candidates.
- Distinguish available balance from ledger balance whenever holds or authorisations exist.
- Card authorisation reserves, capture moves money, settlement nets between institutions; chargebacks can come months later.
- Instant payments are final: checks happen before submission, and timeouts are resolved by status inquiry, never by resending.
- Keep the end-to-end id through every hop; reconciliation depends on it.
- Use clearing or suspense accounts for in-flight cross-shard or cross-system money, and alert on their age.
- Hot accounts need sub-accounts or buffered credits, not optimistic retries.
- Database constraints are your last line of defence: unique keys, CHECKs, deferred balance triggers.
- Lock accounts in a consistent order to avoid deadlocks in transfers.
- Business dates and cut-offs need explicit time zones and holiday calendars.
- Interest accrues daily at high precision and posts with one rounding at period end.
- Audit trails are append-only and separate from application logs.
- Tokenise card numbers and keep raw PANs in a small, isolated vault to shrink PCI scope.
- Treat personal data minimisation and retention rules as design inputs, not afterthoughts.
- Four-eyes approval for large manual adjustments is a control interviewers like to hear.
- Every external integration needs reconciliation against the partner's reports.
- Mention negative balances from chargebacks and fees, and the recovery process.
- Fraud checks need a stand-in policy for when the scoring service is slow or down.
- FX quotes are single-use and expire; acceptance is one conditional update.
- Settlement files must be ingested idempotently and validated (opening + entries = closing).
- Say how you would prove there are no duplicate charges, not just how you prevent them.

## Java and Spring answers
- When asked about `volatile`, say "visibility and ordering, not atomicity" and give the `count++` example.
- For deadlocks, describe lock ordering and how to see the cycle in a thread dump.
- For thread pools, mention bounded queues, rejection policies and Little's Law sizing.
- For virtual threads, say they add concurrency, not speed, and that downstream limits become the bottleneck.
- Mention JEP 491 when pinning comes up: `synchronized` no longer pins from JDK 24.
- For `CompletableFuture`, say where callbacks run and why I/O must not use the common pool.
- For `ConcurrentHashMap`, prefer `compute`/`merge`/`computeIfAbsent` over get-then-put, and keep mapping functions short.
- For records, mention compact constructors, defensive `List.copyOf`, and that they can't be JPA entities.
- For `BigDecimal`, mention string constructors, scale-aware `equals`, and `compareTo` for value equality.
- For GC questions, ask about heap size and latency target before naming a collector.
- For memory issues in containers, mention non-heap memory and `MaxRAMPercentage`.
- For performance, say "measure first": JFR, async-profiler flame graphs, `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`.
- For `@Transactional`, mention proxies, self-invocation, rollback rules and propagation.
- Never put remote calls inside a database transaction; say it before the interviewer asks.
- For JPA, mention N+1 and how you'd detect it (SQL logs, traces) and fix it (fetch join, entity graph, projection).
- Say `open-in-view` should be off for APIs.
- Use Testcontainers, not H2, for database tests, and say why.
- For Spring Security, describe the filter chain and resource-server JWT validation including audience.
- Ownership checks belong in queries (`findByIdAndOwnerId`); mention BOLA by name.
- For Kafka in Spring, mention idempotent consumers, error handlers with a DLT, and observation for tracing.
- For Spring Boot 4, know the headline changes: modular starters, Jackson 3, Framework 7 API versioning and resilience annotations.
- `@MockBean` is gone in Boot 4; say `@MockitoBean`.
- For configuration, prefer `@ConfigurationProperties` records with validation over scattered `@Value`.
- Graceful shutdown and readiness probes are part of a production-ready answer.
- For caching, mention TTLs, stampede protection, and what must never be cached.
- For scheduling across instances, mention `SKIP LOCKED` or ShedLock and idempotent jobs.
- For HTTP clients, always set connect and read timeouts; say the defaults may be infinite.
- For observability, mention Micrometer Observation producing metrics and traces, and correlation ids in logs.
- For errors, return RFC 9457 problem details from a `@RestControllerAdvice`.
- When unsure of an API detail, explain the concept and say you'd check the docs; guessing confidently is worse.

## Coding round
- Restate the problem and confirm inputs, outputs and constraints before writing code.
- List edge cases out loud first: empty input, zero, negative, overflow, duplicates, concurrency, currency mismatch.
- Start with a simple correct solution, then optimise if asked; say the complexity of each.
- Name things in the domain's language (`debit`, `allocate`, `settle`), not `doIt` or `temp`.
- Use immutable value types (records) for inputs and results; it prevents a class of bugs and reads well.
- Validate arguments at the boundary with clear exceptions.
- Use `Math.addExact` for money arithmetic and mention why.
- In concurrency problems, state the invariant you're protecting before choosing a lock or atomic.
- Prefer the smallest correct synchronisation: one lock with a clear scope beats clever lock-free code.
- For transfers, lock in a global order and explain why it prevents deadlock.
- For rate limiters, inject a clock so the solution is testable, and say so.
- For caches, bound the size and explain eviction.
- Write at least two tests: the happy path and the nastiest edge case.
- For concurrency, describe how you'd test it: many threads, a start latch, an invariant, repeated runs.
- Talk while you code, but in short sentences; silence for minutes is hard to score.
- If stuck, solve a smaller version first and tell the interviewer what you're trying.
- Don't over-engineer with interfaces and patterns the problem doesn't need.
- Use standard library structures by name with their complexity (`ArrayDeque`, `TreeMap.ceilingEntry`).
- Know your collection complexities: HashMap O(1) average, TreeMap O(log n), ArrayList add amortised O(1).
- Handle `InterruptedException` properly in any blocking code: restore the flag.
- Avoid `synchronized` on public or shared objects you don't own; lock on private final fields.
- When asked "is this thread-safe?", identify shared mutable state and how each access is protected.
- Prefer early returns and small methods; readability is scored.
- Check your solution against the edge cases you listed before saying you're done.
- State the time and space complexity without being asked.
- Mention how the solution behaves under a production failure (crash mid-way, retry, duplicate input).
- If the interviewer hints, take the hint; arguing for your first approach costs time.
- Leave a minute to summarise what you'd improve with more time (persistence, metrics, more tests).
- Practise typing Java without an IDE's autocomplete; small syntax stalls add up.
- Rehearse five patterns until they're automatic: idempotent store, ordered locking, token bucket, sliding window, retry with jitter.
