## Tips 1-150

### System design framing

1. Spend the first five minutes on requirements and say them back: functional, non-functional (latency, availability, consistency), and what's out of scope.
2. Ask "can this operation be executed twice?" early in any payments design; it shows you know where fintech systems break.
3. State assumptions aloud with numbers ("assume 10 million users, 2 payments each per day") so the interviewer can correct them before you build on them.
4. Do the estimation arithmetic on the board; the method matters more than the exact number.
5. Convert daily volume to per-second by dividing by about 100,000, then apply a stated peak factor.
6. Let the estimate drive a decision ("this fits one PostgreSQL primary"); an estimate that changes nothing wastes time.
7. Draw the data model before the boxes: tables, keys and constraints reveal correctness thinking.
8. Put money in minor units with a currency column in your schema sketch; interviewers notice.
9. Separate the synchronous accept path from asynchronous processing in your first diagram.
10. Name the source of truth for every piece of data; ambiguity there is where consistency bugs hide.
11. Define the API with idempotency keys, status codes and error format; it's quick and high-signal.
12. Reach the deep dive by minute 25 of 45; if you're still drawing boxes, cut the diagram to five components.
13. Pick the deep dive where money can be lost or duplicated, or ask the interviewer which part matters most to them.
14. Walk the failure path, not just the happy path: "the PSP times out; here's what happens."
15. Add a failure-mode table (failure, detection, mitigation, blast radius) near the end; it summarises resilience thinking.
16. Mention SLIs and SLOs for the system you designed; it shows you think about operating it.
17. Close with a 60-second summary and the risks you would tackle next with more time.
18. When the interviewer changes a requirement mid-way, restate the change and show what it breaks in your design.
19. If you don't know a specific technology, describe the mechanism you need instead ("a log with per-key ordering").
20. Use the interviewer as a collaborator: "Would you like me to go deeper on consistency or on scaling?"
21. Keep diagrams legible: label arrows with what flows (command, event, query) and sync vs async.
22. Write the read and write paths separately when they have different scale or consistency needs.
23. Show how a request is traced end to end (correlation id) when you discuss operations.
24. Mention security boundaries early: who authenticates, where authorisation happens, where card data lives.
25. For multi-region questions, ask about data residency and RPO/RTO before choosing active-active.
26. Prefer boring, proven technology in your design and justify any exotic choice with a requirement.
27. Say what you would measure before optimising; it signals engineering discipline.
28. When asked "how would this scale 10×?", find the first bottleneck rather than listing every scaling technique.
29. Don't design for a billion users when the requirement is a hundred thousand; over-engineering is a signal too.
30. Practise the round on a clock; timing problems are invisible until you rehearse under pressure.

### Architecture trade-offs

31. Phrase every choice as "X because Y; the cost is Z; I'd revisit if W."
32. Compare at least two options on the dimensions that matter for this problem, not generic pros and cons.
33. Strong consistency where money moves, eventual consistency where it's only shown: say which is which.
34. A modular monolith is a legitimate senior answer when the domain is young and the team is small.
35. Microservices trade single-database transactions for independence; name what replaces those transactions (sagas, outbox, reconciliation).
36. Synchronous calls couple availability; events couple schemas. Say which coupling you accept and why.
37. Caching buys latency at the price of staleness; never cache what a spending decision depends on.
38. Sharding buys write scale at the price of cross-shard transactions; choose the key so most operations stay single-shard.
39. Kafka is a log, not a queue: it gives replay and ordering per partition, not per-message acknowledgement and priorities.
40. Read replicas scale reads but add lag; keep post-write reads and balance checks on the primary.
41. Event sourcing gives a perfect audit history but makes queries, schema evolution and erasure harder; use it where history is the product.
42. CQRS is worth it when read and write shapes really differ; otherwise it's two models to maintain.
43. Optimistic locking suits low contention; pessimistic locking suits hot rows. Name the contention you expect.
44. Exactly-once delivery doesn't exist across networks; exactly-once effects do, with idempotency.
45. Sagas give availability but visible intermediate states; mention semantic locks or holds to manage them.
46. A workflow engine (Temporal) earns its keep when you have many long-running processes; for one simple flow, a persisted state machine is fine.
47. Distributed SQL buys consistency at scale with consensus latency; sharded PostgreSQL keeps latency low but pushes complexity into your code.
48. Build vs buy: buy commodity (KYC checks, email), build differentiators, and keep an interface so you can switch.
49. Every new technology adds operational cost; count on-call, upgrades and expertise in your trade-off.
50. Timeouts trade completeness for availability; a fast failure is usually better than a slow success nobody waits for.
51. Retries trade load for success rate; cap them with budgets and make the operation idempotent first.
52. Fail open or fail closed depends on whether the control protects fairness or safety; say which.
53. Active-active multi-region trades simplicity and consistency for availability; active-passive is often enough for a ledger.
54. Denormalising for reads is fine if one place owns the truth and the copies are rebuildable.
55. Mention reversibility: prefer decisions that are cheap to undo, and spend design time on the ones that aren't.
56. Use ADRs to show decisions are recorded with context; it's a staff-level habit.
57. Explain how you'd validate a trade-off with a prototype or load test instead of debating it.
58. Consider the team: a design the team can't operate is worse than a simpler one it can.
59. Data gravity matters: compute moving to data is often cheaper than data moving to compute.
60. Name the constraint you're optimising for (latency, cost, correctness, time to market) before choosing.

### Fintech correctness

61. Never use `double` for money; say minor units or `BigDecimal` with explicit rounding, and why.
62. Allocation must not lose a cent: mention the largest-remainder method for splits.
63. Round once, at a defined boundary, with a documented rule.
64. Every money movement is a balanced journal entry; balances are derived and cached, never edited directly.
65. Corrections are reversals plus new entries, with a reason and an actor.
66. A timeout from a PSP or bank is an unknown outcome; say "UNKNOWN, resolve by status query and reconciliation."
67. Idempotency keys belong in the same transaction as the business change, under a unique constraint.
68. Same idempotency key with a different body must be rejected, not replayed.
69. Reconciliation is the proof the system works; describe matching passes and typed breaks.
70. Never auto-match ambiguous reconciliation candidates.
71. Distinguish available balance from ledger balance whenever holds or authorisations exist.
72. Card authorisation reserves, capture moves money, settlement nets between institutions; chargebacks can come months later.
73. Instant payments are final: checks happen before submission, and timeouts are resolved by status inquiry, never by resending.
74. Keep the end-to-end id through every hop; reconciliation depends on it.
75. Use clearing or suspense accounts for in-flight cross-shard or cross-system money, and alert on their age.
76. Hot accounts need sub-accounts or buffered credits, not optimistic retries.
77. Database constraints are your last line of defence: unique keys, CHECKs, deferred balance triggers.
78. Lock accounts in a consistent order to avoid deadlocks in transfers.
79. Business dates and cut-offs need explicit time zones and holiday calendars.
80. Interest accrues daily at high precision and posts with one rounding at period end.
81. Audit trails are append-only and separate from application logs.
82. Tokenise card numbers and keep raw PANs in a small, isolated vault to shrink PCI scope.
83. Treat personal data minimisation and retention rules as design inputs, not afterthoughts.
84. Four-eyes approval for large manual adjustments is a control interviewers like to hear.
85. Every external integration needs reconciliation against the partner's reports.
86. Mention negative balances from chargebacks and fees, and the recovery process.
87. Fraud checks need a stand-in policy for when the scoring service is slow or down.
88. FX quotes are single-use and expire; acceptance is one conditional update.
89. Settlement files must be ingested idempotently and validated (opening + entries = closing).
90. Say how you would prove there are no duplicate charges, not just how you prevent them.

### Java and Spring answers

91. When asked about `volatile`, say "visibility and ordering, not atomicity" and give the `count++` example.
92. For deadlocks, describe lock ordering and how to see the cycle in a thread dump.
93. For thread pools, mention bounded queues, rejection policies and Little's Law sizing.
94. For virtual threads, say they add concurrency, not speed, and that downstream limits become the bottleneck.
95. Mention JEP 491 when pinning comes up: `synchronized` no longer pins from JDK 24.
96. For `CompletableFuture`, say where callbacks run and why I/O must not use the common pool.
97. For `ConcurrentHashMap`, prefer `compute`/`merge`/`computeIfAbsent` over get-then-put, and keep mapping functions short.
98. For records, mention compact constructors, defensive `List.copyOf`, and that they can't be JPA entities.
99. For `BigDecimal`, mention string constructors, scale-aware `equals`, and `compareTo` for value equality.
100. For GC questions, ask about heap size and latency target before naming a collector.
101. For memory issues in containers, mention non-heap memory and `MaxRAMPercentage`.
102. For performance, say "measure first": JFR, async-profiler flame graphs, `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`.
103. For `@Transactional`, mention proxies, self-invocation, rollback rules and propagation.
104. Never put remote calls inside a database transaction; say it before the interviewer asks.
105. For JPA, mention N+1 and how you'd detect it (SQL logs, traces) and fix it (fetch join, entity graph, projection).
106. Say `open-in-view` should be off for APIs.
107. Use Testcontainers, not H2, for database tests, and say why.
108. For Spring Security, describe the filter chain and resource-server JWT validation including audience.
109. Ownership checks belong in queries (`findByIdAndOwnerId`); mention BOLA by name.
110. For Kafka in Spring, mention idempotent consumers, error handlers with a DLT, and observation for tracing.
111. For Spring Boot 4, know the headline changes: modular starters, Jackson 3, Framework 7 API versioning and resilience annotations.
112. `@MockBean` is gone in Boot 4; say `@MockitoBean`.
113. For configuration, prefer `@ConfigurationProperties` records with validation over scattered `@Value`.
114. Graceful shutdown and readiness probes are part of a production-ready answer.
115. For caching, mention TTLs, stampede protection, and what must never be cached.
116. For scheduling across instances, mention `SKIP LOCKED` or ShedLock and idempotent jobs.
117. For HTTP clients, always set connect and read timeouts; say the defaults may be infinite.
118. For observability, mention Micrometer Observation producing metrics and traces, and correlation ids in logs.
119. For errors, return RFC 9457 problem details from a `@RestControllerAdvice`.
120. When unsure of an API detail, explain the concept and say you'd check the docs; guessing confidently is worse.

### Coding round

121. Restate the problem and confirm inputs, outputs and constraints before writing code.
122. List edge cases out loud first: empty input, zero, negative, overflow, duplicates, concurrency, currency mismatch.
123. Start with a simple correct solution, then optimise if asked; say the complexity of each.
124. Name things in the domain's language (`debit`, `allocate`, `settle`), not `doIt` or `temp`.
125. Use immutable value types (records) for inputs and results; it prevents a class of bugs and reads well.
126. Validate arguments at the boundary with clear exceptions.
127. Use `Math.addExact` for money arithmetic and mention why.
128. In concurrency problems, state the invariant you're protecting before choosing a lock or atomic.
129. Prefer the smallest correct synchronisation: one lock with a clear scope beats clever lock-free code.
130. For transfers, lock in a global order and explain why it prevents deadlock.
131. For rate limiters, inject a clock so the solution is testable, and say so.
132. For caches, bound the size and explain eviction.
133. Write at least two tests: the happy path and the nastiest edge case.
134. For concurrency, describe how you'd test it: many threads, a start latch, an invariant, repeated runs.
135. Talk while you code, but in short sentences; silence for minutes is hard to score.
136. If stuck, solve a smaller version first and tell the interviewer what you're trying.
137. Don't over-engineer with interfaces and patterns the problem doesn't need.
138. Use standard library structures by name with their complexity (`ArrayDeque`, `TreeMap.ceilingEntry`).
139. Know your collection complexities: HashMap O(1) average, TreeMap O(log n), ArrayList add amortised O(1).
140. Handle `InterruptedException` properly in any blocking code: restore the flag.
141. Avoid `synchronized` on public or shared objects you don't own; lock on private final fields.
142. When asked "is this thread-safe?", identify shared mutable state and how each access is protected.
143. Prefer early returns and small methods; readability is scored.
144. Check your solution against the edge cases you listed before saying you're done.
145. State the time and space complexity without being asked.
146. Mention how the solution behaves under a production failure (crash mid-way, retry, duplicate input).
147. If the interviewer hints, take the hint; arguing for your first approach costs time.
148. Leave a minute to summarise what you'd improve with more time (persistence, metrics, more tests).
149. Practise typing Java without an IDE's autocomplete; small syntax stalls add up.
150. Rehearse five patterns until they're automatic: idempotent store, ordered locking, token bucket, sliding window, retry with jitter.
